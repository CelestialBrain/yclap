## Key findings

### 1. Law design vs. on-ground compliance

Republic Act No. 9003 (Ecological Solid Waste Management Act of 2000, effectivity 2001) makes **LGUs primarily responsible** for implementation and enforcement (Sec. 10). Core LGU duties:

| Mandate | What RA 9003 requires |
|--------|------------------------|
| Governance | Provincial / city-municipal / barangay Solid Waste Management Boards |
| Planning | 10-year Solid Waste Management Plan (SWMP), NSWMC-approved |
| Diversion | ≥25% diversion from disposal within 5 years of effectivity; goals rise every 3 years thereafter (Sec. 20) |
| Segregation & collection | Barangay: biodegradable, compostable, reusable; city/municipality: residual & special waste |
| Facilities | Materials Recovery Facility (MRF) per barangay or cluster; phase-out of open dumps; sanitary landfills (SLF) for residuals |
| Prohibitions | Open dumping, open burning, mixed collection where segregation is mandated (Sec. 48) |

**Headline outcome after two decades:** solid waste **rose**, not fell. COA’s 2023 performance audit of the Solid Waste Management Program found national generation moved from **9.07 million metric tons (MMT) in 2000 to 16.63 MMT in 2020**, with projections of **~19.8 MMT by 2030** and **24.5 MMT by 2045**. Auditors concluded the program “may not be seen as progressively achieving its goals,” citing inconsistent segregation/diversion and **mixed waste arriving at landfills**, which shortens SLF life and undercuts MRF diversion capacity.

EMB Solid Waste Management Division dashboard data (cited May 2025 in GIZ Urban-Act/DILG policy brief) put generation even higher in recent official tracking: on the order of **~21 MMT (2020 baseline) rising toward ~22.9 MMT by 2025**. DENR public messaging (2025–2026) cites roughly **61,000 metric tons/day** nationally.

There is **no single official “% of LGUs fully compliant with RA 9003” score for 2020–2026**. Compliance is tracked **by component** (boards, plans, MRFs, SLFs, diversion). Component rates remain well below statutory intent.

### 2. LGU compliance rates by component (2020–2026 window)

#### A. 10-year SWMP approval (closest thing to an NSWMC “scorecard”)

NSWMC/EMB track approval status of LGU 10-year plans (public dashboard + GIZ synthesis of NSWMC data):

| Snapshot | Approved SWMPs | Share of LGUs | Notes |
|----------|----------------|---------------|--------|
| **27 Jul 2022** | **1,175** of 1,716 | **68%** | NCR: 17/18 (94%); weak: Region 5 (33%), BARMM (35%), 4B (49%) |
| **Apr 2025** | **927** | **58.23%** | Drop reflects **renewal cycle**, not pure “loss” of plans: **492 (30.9%) for renewal**, **173 (10.8%) returned for completion** |

Interpretation: paper compliance (having an approved plan) is **moderate and fragile**. Plan approval is centralized at NSWMC; renewals create a continuous backlog. A plan on file ≠ funded MRFs, working diversion, or closed dumps.

#### B. Local SWM boards / committees

Older but still-cited SEPO/DENR figures (as of ~2010 inventory, still used in 2025 GIZ brief because fresher national board-activity data are scarce):

| Level | Boards created | Active (approx.) | Active as % of total units |
|-------|----------------|------------------|----------------------------|
| Provinces | 86% created | ~78% of created | **~68%** of provinces active |
| Cities/municipalities | 61% created | ~61% of created | **~37%** of cities/munis active |
| Barangays | 43% created | ~30% of created | **~13%** of barangays active |

Barangays are the statutory front line for segregation and MRF operation; **very low board activity** is a structural compliance gap.

#### C. MRF coverage (national)

| Year / source | MRFs / barangay service | Coverage |
|---------------|-------------------------|----------|
| 2010 (DENR series) | 6,957 MRFs | Baseline growth period |
| 2018 (DENR / NEDA-cited) | 13,612 MRFs; ~30.9% of barangays | Far below 2016 coverage targets (~67–77% in older planning goals) |
| 2020 (NSWMC via World Bank) | 10,722 MRFs | **~33%** of ~42,036 barangays |
| **2021 (COA 2023 audit)** | **11,637 MRFs** servicing **16,418** barangays | **39.05%** of **42,046** barangays |

**Net:** after 20+ years, **most barangays still lack a functioning MRF**. Many EMB-funded MRFs and equipment sets are **non-operational** (COA: ≥₱555.65M / ≥635 units idle as of May 2022—often no electricity; ₱124.22M MRF/equipment funds still outstanding June 2022). Facilities that exist often **stockpile** unprocessed recyclables when markets or logistics fail.

#### D. Sanitary landfill access

| Year / source | Operational SLFs | LGUs served | Share of LGUs |
|---------------|------------------|-------------|---------------|
| Pre-2004 | <4 | — | Near zero |
| 2018 (DENR-EMB) | ~166 | ~21.7% access | — |
| 2020 (NSWMC) | 185 | 378 | **~23%** |
| **2021 (COA)** | **245** | **478 of 1,634** | **29.25%** |
| **Apr 2025 (EMB dashboard via GIZ)** | **366** | **677** | **~40%+** of current LGU count (improving but still majority without access) |

Progress is real (SLF count roughly doubled 2021→2025) but **majority of LGUs still lack SLF access**, which keeps illegal dumping and residual-containment shortcuts alive.

#### E. “Full compliance” estimates from secondary sources

- NEDA (2018, still cited in 2026 MRF literature review): only about **37% of LGUs fully following RA 9003**.
- Regional academic work (e.g. CALABARZON LGU surveys) often reports **partial** compliance (boards and plans on paper; weak segregation, monitoring, and resident cooperation).
- Household/community studies (e.g. Gingoog 2025): majority know the law exists; **minority practice segregation**; majority **dissatisfied** with LGU monitoring.

### 3. Metro Manila MRF coverage (critical for Gargar geography)

World Bank assessment of Metro Manila’s 17 LGUs (using NSWMC plans, MMDA, EMB-NCR data; ~2020–2022 vintage, still the best public NCR facility census cited through 2026 reviews):

| Metric | Figure |
|--------|--------|
| Metro Manila waste | **~9,500 tons/day** (2020 NSWMC) |
| Barangays with own MRF | **334 of 1,710 ≈ 20%** |
| EMB-NCR-funded share | **130 of 334** (typical floor area ~50 m²) |
| Centralized MRFs (metro-scale) | **~13** (200–1,000 m² class), multi-LGU catchments, often composting-oriented |
| City examples | **Manila:** only **3** MRFs + **642** junkshop MRS for **897** barangays; **Quezon City:** 61 MRFs + 84 MRS / 142 barangays; **Caloocan:** 20 MRFs + 120 MRS / 188 barangays |

**Why coverage stays low in NCR:** scarce and expensive land; tiny barangays (especially Manila); preference for **Material Recovery Systems (MRS)**—formal or informal **junkshop partnerships**—over building a shed MRF every barangay. Functionality is uneven: many barangay MRFs handle recyclables only; few process both recyclables and biodegradables; mechanized mixed-waste MRFs (e.g. past Pasig contractor models) are rare and contract-fragile.

**Recycling gap:** World Bank and later reviews note recyclables still riding collection trucks and disposal streams because source segregation and MRF throughput fail. Informal collectors and junkshops **do most real diversion**—a compliance substitute, not a statutory MRF network.

### 4. Open dumps remaining

RA 9003 **prohibits open dumps** (and required controlled dumps to be phased out on a timetable). Reality:

| Period | Signal |
|--------|--------|
| 2008 → 2015 | Open dumps cut roughly in half (**~806 → ~350**) — large historical progress |
| Early national framework inventory | Hundreds of open dumps still slated for closure (older NSWMC framework cited **726**) |
| **2021 DENR campaign** | Secretary ordered **nationwide open-dump closure by March 2021**; EMB publicized mass closures and residual containment alternatives |
| **COA 2023** | Explicit: **illegal dumpsites could not be avoided** in some LGUs because disposal capacity is insufficient |
| **2024–2025** | COA still flags LGUs (e.g. **Aleosan, Cotabato** audit) operating open dumps **despite** a local SLF remaining non-operational; PSA 2025 reporting noted **illegal dumpsite counts rising year-on-year in related waste statistics** (79 reported in 2024 vs 43 in 2023 in that series) |
| Social / advocacy messaging | Older viral figures (e.g. “404 illegal open dumps vs 164 SLFs”) still circulate; treat as **indicative of the gap**, not a verified 2026 inventory |

**Bottom line:** legal open dumps are not “gone.” Closures advanced under political pressure (2021), but **capacity gaps + weak enforcement** mean dumps reappear as **illegal residual sites**, “controlled” dumps that operate as open dumps, or non-operating SLFs. Residual Containment Areas (RCAs) are a stopgap, not RA 9003 full compliance.

### 5. NSWMC “scorecards” and monitoring instruments

NSWMC does **not** publish a single letter-grade national LGU scorecard equivalent to a school report card. What exists:

1. **Dashboard tracking** (nswmc.emb.gov.ph): waste generation projections, SLF counts, SWMP approval/renewal status — the operational “scoreboard.”
2. **LGU-SWM-SCMAR** (Self-Compliance Monitoring and Auditing Report): standardized self-audit form (MRF location, wastes processed, household hazardous waste, etc.) — **self-reported**, variable quality.
3. **10-year SWMP evaluation & NSWMC en banc approval** of LGU plans (resolution + certificate) — binary “approved / for revision / for renewal.”
4. **Third-party audits:** COA performance audit (2023) is the strongest independent national assessment in the 2020–2026 window.
5. **Programmatic assessments:** World Bank Metro Manila SWM study used multi-criteria LGU tiering (plastic ordinances, diversion facilities, MRS, etc.) — research scorecard, not statutory NSWMC grades.

**Gap:** self-compliance tools without **independent verification, public league tables, or automatic sanctions** produce optimistic paperwork and weak outcomes.

### 6. Penalties on the books — rarely enforced

**Statutory penalties (RA 9003 Sec. 48–49)** escalate by offense:

| Offense class (illustrative) | Penalty range |
|------------------------------|---------------|
| Littering / minor Sec. 48(1) | Fine **₱300–₱1,000** and/or community service 1–15 days |
| Related minor prohibitions | Fine ₱300–₱1,000 and/or jail 1–15 days |
| Mid-tier (open burning, etc.) | Fine ₱1,000–₱3,000 and/or jail 15 days–6 months |
| Open dumping / facility violations / serious Sec. 48(8–11) | **₱500,000** + **5–10% of net annual income**; imprisonment **1–3 years** on subsequent violations for some paragraphs |

**Why enforcement is weak (convergent finding across COA, GIZ, SSRN penalty analysis, envi NGOs):**

- **Primary enforcer is the LGU** that is often itself non-compliant (political cost of fining voters or peer officials).
- Household fines are **too small** to change behavior; industrial/open-dump fines require prosecution capacity few ENROs have.
- **National Solid Waste Management Fund (NSWMF)**—meant to be fed partly by fines, penalties, and permits—was **still not activated** as of COA 2023. No reliable fine-revenue flywheel.
- DENR visitorial powers exist (Sec. 9) but **closure campaigns are episodic** (e.g. 2021 dump crackdown) rather than continuous prosecution.
- Academic/legal reviews (2025–2026) flag enforcement as **the single largest weakness** of the RA 9003 penalty system; many LGUs **hesitate to impose fines**.

Result: **law looks strict; practice is admonition + occasional closure order**, not systematic fining of LGUs or generators.

## YCLAP–Gargar implication

1. **Gargar is not competing with a completed RA 9003 system — it is filling a documented hole.** National MRF service ~39% and Metro Manila barangay MRF ~20% mean **most Ateneo / Pasig / NCR catchments still lack real recovery infrastructure**. A scrap-to-value pilot that increases **measured diversion kg** is *de facto* LGU-mandate support, not a side project.

2. **Treat junkshops and informal collectors as the working MRF network.** World Bank, PIDS, and BAN Toxics all show the informal sector bridges collection–segregation–recycling where barangay MRFs fail. Gargar’s price transparency + collector path is **climate justice + RA 9003 functional compliance**, especially as EPR (RA 11898) pulls formal brand money into recovery without automatically formalizing workers.

3. **Do not pitch “carbon credits” as the product; pitch diversion metrics LGUs already owe NSWMC.** RA 9003 already requires diversion tracking. Gargar’s kg diverted, composition, and barangay map can feed **LGU SWMP / SCMAR-style reporting**—useful for SK, MENRO/CENRO, and campaign demos—without waiting for NSWMF or carbon markets.

4. **Organic fraction + landfill methane is the climate hook.** Mixed waste in SLFs (COA finding) is a methane and capacity problem. Food-scrap / wet-waste diversion is both **mitigation** and **flood co-benefit** (basura → baha), aligning with the YCLAP brief’s under-addressed priorities.

5. **Advocacy angle: enforcement theater vs. capacity.** Youth campaigns should pressure for (a) **working MRFs/MRS**, (b) **segregated collection that actually stays segregated**, and (c) **honest open-dump disclosure**—not only “fine the litterer.” COA already said illegal dumps persist **because disposal options are missing**. Gargar should position as **capacity + market**, not moralism.

6. **LGU partner pitch language:** “We help you hit the Sec. 20 diversion trajectory and barangay recovery mandate with transparent scrap markets and youth organizers—without waiting for a 50 m² shed on land you don’t have.” MRS/junkshop partnership models (Manila, Malabon, Caloocan) are **legible to city SWM offices**.

7. **Risk:** LGU “compliance theater” (plan approved, MRF photo-op, equipment idle). Gargar metrics must be **independent and public** (kg, barangay, price) so they cannot be co-opted as greenwash.
