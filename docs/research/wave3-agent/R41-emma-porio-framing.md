### 2. Vulnerability themes (what “vulnerability” means in her work)

Porio’s long arc rejects single-dimension vulnerability (biophysical hazard maps alone). Recurring analytic stack:

1. **Place-based ecological–environmental vulnerability**  
   - Flood plains, riverine/coastal locations, reclaimed marine ponds, below-sea-level zones, land subsidence, inadequate drainage/esteros, “danger zones” policy designations.  
   - Classic case work: Pasig–Marikina basin urban poor; Tanza (Navotas) PCRA — storm/tidal surge, river flooding, siltation, garbage clogging, dikes that can trap water when gates close.

2. **Social / sector-specific vulnerability**  
   - Poverty, tenure insecurity (owner / renter / sharer), access to water–power–health–sanitation, informal livelihoods.  
   - **Gender** as a structuring axis: women-headed households and women’s care/livelihood burdens systematically face higher damages and recovery costs (documented quantitatively in Ondoy-era tables and again in 2024–2025 CCARPH survey work).  
   - Sector differences: urban poor households vs commercial–industrial establishments respond and lose differently under the same flood.

3. **Interaction effect (her signature claim)**  
   - Place-based environmental degradation **interacts with** social vulnerability (income, gender, tenure) to **amplify** flood damages and human-security deficits.  
   - Poor + female-headed + degraded riverside micro-location = systematically higher losses than better-off households in the same barangay.  
   - Implication: **disaggregate** risk by place *and* group; city-level averages hide who actually drowns, loses income, or stays out of school.

4. **Governance & human security as vulnerability drivers**  
   - Weak preparedness, top-down damage assessment that skips community voice, risk communication failure, exclusive planning.  
   - Vulnerability is not “unwillingness to act”; it is **structural lack of support** to interpret and respond to risk.  
   - Justice frame: inclusive, risk-informed governance sharpens public risk perception; weak governance leaves people to meet crisis only at the point of disruption.

5. **Social capital / trust networks as mediators of well-being**  
   - 2025 *Journal of Asian Sociology* paper (Porio, Zulueta, Pascual): gender, SES, and **environmental vulnerability of residence** structure social capital/trust networks (SC/TN) and well-being.  
   - Women (esp. women-headed households in informal settlements) face **disproportionately high climate-related flood damages** but also build/maintain **dense support networks** that buffer pre-/during-/post-disaster access to resources.  
   - Policy implication: integrate **informal** and **formal** resilience; support locally driven adaptation that recognizes these converging pathways.

6. **Climate justice / adaptation pathway lens (recent co-authored work)**  
   - Pathways to **transformative climate justice** (See, Fuentes, Porio, Wilmsen, *Local Environment*, 2025).  
   - **Planned relocation vs in-situ management** compared for justice outcomes of state-led adaptation (See, Wilmsen, Porio, 2024 handbook chapter).  
   - Students should not treat “relocate” as a technical fix; justice outcomes (participation, livelihood, services, voice) are the analytic test.

### 4. Community assessment themes (PCRA / CRA as her method DNA)

**Participatory Community Risk Assessment (PCRA)** is the method students should name correctly if they claim lineage from her work.

**What PCRA is in her practice (Tanza / KAMANAVA model, clinical sociology + CBO):**

1. **Partnership first** — university clinical sociology team + women’s CBO + community council; tripartite MOA; not extractive one-off surveys.  
2. **Capacity transfer** — train community members (esp. women leaders) in FGD, KII, walk-through, life histories, community risk mapping.  
3. **Co-produce risk profile** — ecological–environmental + social–environmental + economic–political factors; gendered risk points on the same map.  
4. **From map to plan** — community-based risk reduction & management plan (CB-RRMP): clean-up, dike/wall maintenance, drainage advocacy, multi-stakeholder council, livelihood/housing asks.  
5. **Power analysis** — PCRA is also about **who gets to define risk** and negotiate resources with LGU/national agencies; community data becomes advocacy capital.  
6. **Time & trust** — her published lessons stress that genuine participation costs weeks/months; openness/transparency builds cooperation; power structures constrain ordinary people.

**Tools / outputs students may reference (public):**

- Community risk maps (hazard + land use + informal vs formal settlement + relocation zones)  
- Gender-disaggregated loss tables (income, school absences, basic services costs, climate-sensitive disease costs)  
- Social Vulnerability Index (SOVI)-style work presented in PSA / well-being measurement contexts for Manila (“who is most vulnerable to climate and disaster risks”)  
- CCARPH Resilience Toolkit / science–policy–practice materials for LGUs and partners  

**What community assessment is *not* in her frame:** remote dashboards without community ownership; pure biophysical flood models; top-down damage inventories done only by city DRRM offices; “solutions” designed in air-conditioned rooms far from women who make a living off the land/water (she has cited this global feminist climate-justice critique approvingly).

## Mentor framing tips

### A. How student projects should *frame* for her as mentor

1. **Lead with people and place, not gadgets.**  
   Open with *who* is exposed *where* (barangay micro-location, tenure, livelihood, gender composition), then introduce any sensor/app/map as a **support** to community-defined risk—not the other way around.

2. **Disaggregate risk.**  
   Explicitly plan gender- and SES-disaggregated outcomes (who loses income days, who cleans mud, who gets last-mile warning). Cite her interaction thesis: place × gender × poverty.

3. **Name assessment as participatory, not extractive.**  
   If doing community work: partnership protocol (CBO/barangay invitation first), training/co-ownership of maps and findings, feedback assembly. Echo PCRA steps in miniature scaled to 5 weeks (do not claim a full Tanza-style 6-week PCRA unless truly resourced).

4. **Connect assessment → advocacy → adaptive capacity.**  
   Her PCRA ends in a **plan** communities can take to LGUs. Student projects should show a plausible path from evidence to barangay/LGU-usable output (risk profile brief, gendered loss storyboard, last-mile warning protocol co-designed with women leaders)—not a terminal demo day artifact.

5. **Justice language over techno-solutionism.**  
   Frame success as stronger community risk perception, clearer governance asks, reduced differential vulnerability—not “we solved flooding.” Engage relocation critically if it appears (livelihood, services, participation).

6. **Honor women’s dual role: high risk + high social capital.**  
   Design for women’s time burdens; measure network/support as adaptive capacity; never only “empowerment theater.”

7. **Transdisciplinary humility.**  
   She partners natural scientists, engineers, LGUs, NRC, IPCC process. Students should show how social science questions discipline the tech stack (and vice versa): “naturalize social science / socialize natural science.”

8. **Philippine urban coastal specificity.**  
   Metro Manila / coastal city examples (flood, heat, polycrisis of water–food–energy–inequality). Avoid generic Global North climate posters.

9. **Honest scale for a 5-week agency.**  
   Respect her published lesson: real PCRA is time- and trust-intensive. Propose a **bounded slice** (one purok mapping module; secondary analysis of public SOVI/CCARPH themes; co-design workshop with an *already partnered* CBO) rather than a citywide resilience master plan.

10. **Service orientation, not saviorism.**  
    Public quotes emphasize service within one’s capacity and *kapit-bisig* multi-sector work. Pitch as contribution to existing community/LGU trajectories, not “our startup fixes the barangay.”

### B. Pitch structure that maps to her priorities

| Pitch beat | Content she will recognize |
|------------|----------------------------|
| Problem | Place-based + social vulnerability interaction in named community/sector |
| Who bears burden | Gender / SES / tenure disaggregation; care vs livelihood risks |
| Gap | Top-down risk assessment or weak risk perception/governance |
| Method | Participatory or co-designed assessment (even if lightweight); mixed methods |
| Output | Community-usable risk profile + justice-aware ask to governance actor |
| Resilience claim | Bounce forward / adaptive capacity / SC-TN strengthened—not only asset recovery |
| Ethics | Invited partnership; women leaders as co-researchers; no extractive data |

### C. What to avoid in mentor meetings / canvases

- Hazard maps with no people, gender, or tenure  
- “Awareness campaign only” without structural/governance analysis  
- Relocation as unexamined good  
- Claiming CCARPH/IPCC affiliation without real linkage  
- Overstating 5-week impact as city transformation  
- Gender as late slide decoration  
- Treating informal settlements only as drainage obstruction rather than rights-bearing communities  
- Ignoring power (who defines risk, who controls dikes/gates/budgets)

### D. Good project archetypes for her mentorship lane

| Archetype | Why it fits |
|-----------|-------------|
| Gender-disaggregated community flood/heat story + map co-owned with women’s group | Direct PCRA / gender lineage |
| Last-mile risk communication designed with social capital networks in mind | Risk perception + SC/TN |
| Justice audit of a local adaptation measure (relocation site vs in-situ upgrade narrative) | 2024–25 justice pathway work |
| Student support to an *invited* barangay CB-RRMP slice (clean-up schedule, care-work-aware warning drill) | Assessment → plan → partnership |
| Polycrisis framing for urban heat/water that ties inequality to risk perception | 2026 interview themes |
| Secondary synthesis of public SOVI / CCARPH toolkit materials into LGU-facing brief for a partner city | Science–policy–practice without overclaiming field access |

### E. Citation etiquette when name-checking her

- Prefer **published** papers and CCARPH/Ateneo public pages over social media paraphrases.  
- Distinguish **her solo classic work** (2011–2014 Metro Manila vulnerability/PCRA) from **co-authored recent** justice and social-capital papers.  
- IPCC Lead Author status = stature, not a blank check to claim student work is “IPCC-aligned.”  
- If using PCRA, cite the method tradition (her Tanza/clinical sociology practice + broader CRA literature she references, e.g. van Aalst et al.).
