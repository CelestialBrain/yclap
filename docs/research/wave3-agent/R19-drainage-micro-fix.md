# R19 — Small-scale drainage & community flood risk reduction (Philippines)

**Scope:** Successful *micro* interventions — barangay canals, rain gardens, cisterns/RWH, youth cleanup + system handoff — **not** mega DPWH/JICA river-channel projects.  
**Lens:** What works at community scale; what fails when handoff/O&M is missing; implication for YCLAP vs Gargar.

---

## Key findings

### 1. Barangay drainage canals work when communities own design → build → O&M

**Balangiga, Eastern Samar (KALAHI-CIDSS / CDD, ~2003–2006)**  
- Residents prioritized a **network of barangay canals** to move floodwater to the sea after ~20 years of knee-deep, multi-day inundation that shut schools/business and drove water-borne skin disease.  
- Of **26** subprojects (≈₱18M total), **14 were drainage/flood control** — communities chose them because floods hit ~**60%** of the population in rainy season.  
- Real-world stress test: after local drainage finished, **Typhoon Feria** brought heavy rain but the town **did not go underwater** as before; residents attributed the change to the canals (“free from the scourge of the floods”).  
- **Success mechanism is process, not concrete alone:** participatory prioritization, local labor/jobs, and LGU uptake of the CDD method (later *Makamasang Tugon*). Sustainability narrative centers on collective ownership, not one-off construction.  
- **Caveat:** reported as qualitative case (World Bank / PreventionWeb feature); not a multi-year hydrologic evaluation.

**Barangay New Panay, Maragusan, Compostela Valley (KALAHI-CIDSS / NCDDP, ~2014)**  
- **200 linear-meter drainage canal**, designed/procured/implemented with community volunteers under the Community Empowerment Activity Cycle; formal **turnover + blessing** to barangay.  
- Prior problem: recurrent overflow into **classrooms** → absenteeism → framed as poverty-cycle break (BSPMC chair: “Students are our investment…”).  
- Illustrates the **handoff pattern** KALAHI bakes in: Barangay Sub-Project Management Committee + **O&M committee** after turnover; mayor/barangay present at handoff.

**Program-level pattern (KALAHI-CIDSS / NCDDP)**  
- Thousands of small subprojects nationwide (MCC/World Bank summaries: drainage systems among farm-to-market roads, water, schools).  
- Evaluations stress that **O&M is satisfactory when** there is a plan, funds, a competent O&M committee, supportive local rules, and regular resident/LGU monitoring — shared responsibility with LGU counterpart funds.  
- **Implication for YCLAP:** a one-day canal dig without an O&M owner is theater; the durable PH model is **committee + barangay budget line + monitoring**, not volunteer heroics alone.

### 2. Rain gardens: strong modeled effect; few true barangay-built pilots

**Modeled — Barangays 659 & 659-A, Ermita, Manila (FEU-IT / WAJES 2020)**  
- EPA **SWMM** study of LID rain gardens on ~14 sub-catchments (high impervious commercial/institutional zone near Manila City Hall).  
- Calibrated to **Typhoon Ondoy (Ketsana)** Port Area rainfall; rain garden assigned to **10%** of each sub-catchment.  
- Result: ponded flood **volume reduced ~38–54%** vs base drainage-only design (max ~53.5%, min ~38.3%); ANOVA significant (F > Fcrit).  
- Authors note results assume drainage remains uncompromised — i.e. **waste clogging cancels LID gains**; recommend regular cleaning + waste control.  
- **Status:** research/simulation, **not** a completed barangay capital project.

**Built (estate / LGU micro, not “mega DPWH”)**  
- **Vertis North Rain Garden (QC, Ayala):** ~2 ha planted detention/infiltration park; cited capacity **~3,000 m³** stormwater; dual use recreation + mini-sponge for the estate. Private master-plan scale, not barangay volunteer-built, but demonstrates PH rain-garden as real infrastructure.  
- **Streetside / campus rain gardens** appear in NbS reviews (WTA Labs 2025) as **barangay/LGU-appropriate** blue-green tools (curbside gardens, bioswales) — low skill barrier, multi-benefit (heat + runoff) — but published **before/after flood metrics at barangay scale remain thin**.

### 3. Cisterns & rainwater harvesting as dual flood + water tools

- **Distributed RWH / cisterns** appear more often as **water security** pilots than as measured urban flood control in PH media; flood co-benefit is logical (roof runoff intercepted) but rarely quantified locally.  
- **Quezon City multipurpose micro-detention** pairs storage with RWH:  
  - Brgy **Santa Monica (Novaliches):** basketball court over underground detention basin **~1,700 m³**.  
  - Brgy **Tandang Sora:** second court-basin **~1,256 m³** catchment + **~15,000 L** rainwater harvest for cleaning/landscaping.  
  - QMC retention/detention pond under construction: cited **~651 m³**, linked to plant watering tanks.  
  These are **LGU dual-use micro-infra** (sports + storage), not mega river projects — good analogy for “small structural + social amenity,” still above pure student build budget.  
- **Malabon** and other LGUs have installed **rainwater harvesting facilities** (WTA Labs cites Malabon City RWH); framed mainly as adaptation / non-potable supply with secondary runoff relief.  
- **DOST-7 Integrated Storm Water Management (Cebu)** demonstrated campus-scale rainwater + stormwater storage tanks (e.g. PhilSci High Argao, Zapatera ES) — useful **school handoff** precedent: facility stays with institution O&M.  
- **Surigao del Sur upland communities:** academic work on rainwater catchment + cooperation as flood resiliency factor (cooperation > hardware alone).

### 4. Youth cleanup is common; **system handoff** is the scarce success factor

**What exists everywhere**  
- Barangay/SK/youth estero cleanups (Tondo LP4Y, Pasay Estero Tripa de Gallena, Laoag youth-led drives, ICC coastal cleanups, Dapitan Adopt-an-Estero sacks, etc.).  
- National pattern: **plastic and debris clog drainage** is a primary Metro Manila flood aggravator (Talayan Creek / ridge-to-reef narrative; MMDA pumping stations clogged with furniture/garbage). Presidential/MMDA drainage declog drives are large-scale versions of the same diagnosis.

**Handoff models that outlast a cleanup day**  
| Model | What is handed off | Why it lasts (when it does) |
|-------|--------------------|-----------------------------|
| **KALAHI O&M committee** | Physical canal/asset + maintenance plan | Barangay assembly reporting; LGU counterpart; trained volunteers |
| **DENR Adopt-an-Estero / Water Body** | Segment of creek/estero | MoA: community + **donor-partner** + LGU + EMB; regular cleanups + greening; private sector funding continuity |
| **LGU dual-use detention** | Basin under public court/park | City engineering O&M; amenity creates political demand to keep it working |
| **School / campus RWH or LID** | Tank/garden on institutional land | Facilities office owns asset; students rotate as labor only |
| **One-off youth cleanup** | Trash sacks | **No owner of the next clog** → relapse within one wet season |

**Design lesson:** successful PH micro-drainage stories are **asset + committee + schedule + budget**, not photo-ops. Youth energy is best as **activation labor + monitoring/scoreboard**, with barangay or LGU holding the system.

### 5. Cross-cutting synthesis (what “success” actually means)

1. **Scale honesty:** Barangay canals solve *local ponding and conveyance*; they do **not** replace watershed peak flows (Marikina/Pasig, Sierra Madre runoff). NbS reviews stress ridge-to-reef / watershed framing.  
2. **Waste is the equalizer:** Even good drainage or modeled rain gardens fail if esteros re-clog — which is why waste diversion (Gargar-class work) is a **flood co-benefit**, not a side quest.  
3. **Photogenic ≠ durable:** Rank risk for student projects is high engagement, low permanence unless handoff is designed in week 1.  
4. **Best micro stacks for youth:**  
   - Cleanup **mapped to a named drain segment** + barangay O&M pledge + monthly monitoring checklist;  
   - **Campus rain garden / cistern demo** with facilities handoff;  
   - **Evidence pack** (before/after photos, kg debris, flood-depth anecdotes) for LGU pitch — not a claim to “solve flooding.”

## YCLAP ranking vs Gargar

| Dimension | Gargar (default flagship) | Drainage micro-fix / cleanup + handoff |
|-----------|---------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Deep-research rank (prior brief)** | **#1** | **#8** — “Photogenic but weak alone” |
| **Student agency (5 weeks)** | High (app + rates + collector path + kg log) | Medium — one cleanup easy; real canal/O&M hard |
| **Measurable pilot proof** | Users, kg diverted, collector links | Debris kg, photos; flood reduction rarely attributable |
| **Climate / justice story** | Methane + circular economy + informal workers | Local flood aggravation + health; weaker methane link |
| **Season fit (Aug–Sep wet)** | Excellent (waste→clogged drains narrative) | Excellent visually; attribution noisy in peak rain |
| **Risk** | Low–med (data honesty, partners) | **Med–high** if sold as flood “solution” without handoff |
| **Mentor fit** | Porio (justice), Lasco (systems), Tan (canvas) | Porio if community-based assessment; Tan if LGU MoA real |
| **Recommended role** | **Primary product** | **Co-benefit module** or **Campaign Canvas evidence**, not replacement flagship |

**Verdict for YCLAP:** Keep **Gargar as flagship**. Use drainage micro cases as:

1. **Narrative bridge** — “clogged drains are a waste problem; diversion is flood adaptation.”  
2. **Optional stretch** — one mapped cleanup + barangay handoff checklist **only if** a partner barangay signs O&M ownership before showcase.  
3. **Do not** pitch student-built canals/rain gardens as flood control without engineering LGU partner and multi-month O&M plan — that is where PH “success” cases actually spent their time.

If the cohort pivots off Gargar toward flood: prefer **early-action playbook (#3 in prior ranking)** or **mapped cleanup + handoff** over structural builds; structural micro-infra is LGU/KALAHI scale, not 5-session build.
