## Key findings

### 1. Scale of the problem (use these numbers carefully)

| Metric | Figure | Notes / vintage |
|--------|--------|-----------------|
| National ISF stock | **~3.7 million** informal settler families | UN-Habitat Philippines Country Report 2023; DHSUD media (2022) |
| NCR high-risk / slum-condition ISF | **~500,000 families** in slums and high-risk areas (railways, waterways, esteros, other danger zones) | UN-Habitat 2023; Philstar / DHSUD Sep 2022 |
| Waterway / “danger area” target (classic Oplan LIKAS frame) | **~104,000 households** along riverbanks, floodways, creeks, esteros identified for resettlement | DILG Oplan Likas update (2013), widely cited (IIED ACCR WP 29, 2016); ~19,440 of these then flagged as *over water* for immediate move |
| Broader programme framing | **~120,000 ISFs** from flood-prone danger zones (Oplan LIKAS 2011–2016 design) | Academic reviews of Oplan LIKAS |
| National share in danger-prone areas | **~51%** of informal settlers in the Philippines located in danger-prone areas (2017 statistics cited in research) | Du et al. / MDPI Sustainability 2020 (on-site upgrading study) |
| Housing backlog (context) | **6.5 M** (2022) → projected **22 M** by 2040 | UN-Habitat 2023 |
| Recent waterway relocation claim | **57,134 ISFs** relocated from flood-prone / waterway–easement areas under Manila Bay Clean-up, Rehabilitation and Preservation Program (MBCRPP), as of mid-2025 DILG/PNA reporting | Government press line (2025); treat as programme count, not “problem solved” |
| Older urban share estimates | **20–35%** of NCR residents in informal settlements (historical secondary literature); older HUDCC 2008-style estimates put millions of *people* in NCR slums | Definitions of “informal settlement” vary; prefer **family/household** official counts when pitching |

**Hazard context:** Metro Manila sits on a flood plain between Manila Bay and Laguna de Bay; Pasig–Marikina–Tullahan systems overflow under intense rain and typhoons. NDRRMC-class reporting commonly notes several major flood episodes per year affecting thousands of families. Informal settlements concentrate exposure because land markets and tenure insecurity push the urban poor onto riverbanks, esteros, dumps, rail corridors, and other “danger areas” defined under **RA 7279 (UDHA)** §28 (esteros, railroad tracks, dumps, river banks, shorelines, waterways, sidewalks, etc.).

**Vulnerability stack (why flood depth ≠ only risk):**

- Insecure tenure → low incentive / ability to invest in safer housing  
- Makeshift materials + density → rapid loss of assets and shelter  
- Weak water, sanitation, solid waste → health cascade after flood; waste also clogs drainage (basura → baha link)  
- Informal livelihoods (daily wage, vending) → every flooded day is lost income with thin savings  
- Social vulnerability multiplies physical flood hazard (elderly, women, children, PWDs under-served by generic alerts)

**YCLAP pitch rule:** Cite **~3.7 M national / ~0.5 M NCR high-risk / ~104k waterway danger-area** as *order-of-magnitude public figures with year tags*. Do not invent a single “current census” of riverside ISFs; counts are political and definition-dependent.

### 2. Relocation issues (why “just move them” fails)

**Policy backbone**

- Post-Ondoy (2009) flood management made **clearing waterways** a core Metro Manila flood strategy.  
- **P50-billion** Alternative Housing Programme / People’s Plan fund (Aquino era) targeted ISFs in NCR danger areas, with formal preference for **on-site, in-city, near-city** relocation *in accordance with a People’s Plan* (JMC 2013 guidelines).  
- **Oplan LIKAS** (*Lumikas para Iwas Kalamidad at Sakit*) operationalized large-scale resettlement (~104k–120k design target). In practice a large share of units were still **off-city** (World Bank RPF material cited ~**76%** of targeted ISFs relocated off-city under early Oplan LIKAS implementation, constrained by land, cost, and agency capacity).  
- Manila Bay clean-up and related waterway programmes continue demolition + relocation logic into the 2020s (including the 57k+ MBCRPP claim).

**Documented failure modes (repeated across ethnography and policy reviews)**

1. **Spatial mismatch / livelihood exile** — Off-city sites (e.g. distant Laguna, Rizal, Cavite patterns) put workers 20–40+ km from Metro Manila jobs. Daily wage jobs do not survive long commutes; families return to the city, rent out or informally sell rights to units, or split households (one earner back in Manila).  
2. **Missing services at “safe” sites** — Incomplete water, transport, schools, clinics, and markets turn housing into a poverty trap; PCUP/OGP-style critiques note downward spiral when social preparation (community organizing / community development) is skipped.  
3. **Amortization stress** — Without stable income, monthly payments fail; return migration re-creates unregulated settlements.  
4. **Social capital rupture** — Neighbourhood networks that provide childcare, credit, job leads, and disaster mutual aid are broken; new HOAs take months/years to rebuild weaker ties.  
5. **Coercion vs “voluntary” framing** — Eviction pressure before rainy seasons, infrastructure projects, and waterway clearing create take-it-or-leave-it choices; “choice” of off-city vs stay in danger is not free consent.  
6. **On-site / People’s Plan friction** — Community-driven People’s Plans raised agency and citizenship (IIED study) but hit multi-agency red tape, budget politics, and agency mandate silos; in-city land is scarce and contested.  
7. **Re-exposure risk** — Poor site selection can recreate flood or other hazard exposure; treating resettlement as a one-off construction event without livelihood and DRRM follow-through fails.

**Urban poor movement consensus (durable):** prioritize **on-site upgrading, in-city / near-city** options; no demolition without adequate relocation; livelihood and services as core design, not add-ons. High-density mid-rise (SHFC HDH-type) emerged partly as a pro-poor compromise to stay near original settlements.

**Implication for students:** Flood safety is **housing + livelihood + tenure + governance**, not only hydrology. A 5-week project cannot “relocate” anyone and should not cosplay as NHA/DHSUD.

### 3. Community-based DRRM (what works at barangay / ISF scale)

**Legal anchor:** **RA 10121** (PDRRM Act 2010) + **RA 9729** (Climate Change Act) put LGUs—and barangays—on the front line for DRRM/CCA plans, BDRRMCs, and local risk assessment. Capacity is highly uneven across NCR’s many barangays.

**What “CBDRRM / CBDRM” means here:** At-risk people identify, plan, implement, monitor, and evaluate risk reduction—not only post-disaster response. Pre-disaster prevention, mitigation, preparedness, inclusive early warning, and local ownership are the point (CADRI / UNDP good-practice framing).

**Metro Manila–relevant practice patterns**

| Practice | Evidence / example | Student-adjacent lesson |
|----------|--------------------|-------------------------|
| Participatory risk & capacity mapping | HVCA / VCA-style tools; community flood maps integrating local knowledge | Maps owned by community beat remote “hero sensors” without trust |
| Community early warning (end-to-end) | NCDP-linked CBDRM in **115 barangays / 10 Metro Manila cities** for ISFs exposed to storm, flood, landslide: HOAs + women/elderly reps; flood drills; EWS with local monitoring & BDRRMC budget ratification (CADRI compendium) | Last-mile alert design + drills + budget line > gadget drop |
| BDRRMC formalization | Barangay council ratification + DRM budget allocation | Institutionalize, don’t invent parallel student “task forces” |
| Case research sites | e.g. **Barangay Tumana, Marikina** (UP CIDS resilient informal settlement work); riverside ISF studies in QC/Marikina/Pasig/Pasay | Use published cases; do not cold-enter for “data extraction” |
| Donor/LGU CBDRM programmes | e.g. BRACE / PRC CBDRM components (Taguig high-exposure barangays historically); RURBANISE-style multi-settlement resilience research | Partner through existing programmes when invited |
| On-site upgrading as resilience | Du et al. (2020) and related work argue on-site upgrading can be viable vs default eviction for hazard-prone ISFs | Align with housing rights framing, not clearance narrative |
| Waste–flood co-benefit | Informal settlement waste + clogged esteros amplify inundation | Strong fit with YCLAP waste/Gargar lane *without* claiming to “fix settlements” |

**What CBDRRM is *not*:** One-day drills without follow-up; outsider risk ranking that overrides community priorities; tech pilots that leave maintenance burden on the poorest; using “resilience” language to justify eviction.

**5-week student-fit wedges (only with invitation):**

- Inclusive **early-action playbooks** (Tagalog/local, PWD/elder-friendly) co-designed with BDRRMC / HOA  
- Drill support materials, contingency card design, or barangay IEC refresh  
- Documentation of **existing** community practice for advocacy (with consent)  
- Waste diversion / estero clog reduction that the community already prioritizes  
- Ally work with LGU DRRMO or CSO already present (not dual power)

**Low / no-go for 5 weeks:** Housing construction, land negotiation, census of ISFs for government clearing, unsolicited mapping that could aid eviction, claiming “we made the community resilient.”

### 4. Cross-cutting synthesis for YCLAP

- **Urgency is very high; student agency is medium and conditional** (matches main deep-research brief: informal settlement risk = very high urgency, **invited partnership only**).  
- Prefer projects that **reduce daily flood co-drivers** (waste, info, early action) or **amplify existing community DRRM**, not structural housing solutions.  
- Never pitch “clear the riverbanks” as a student climate win.  
- Center **justice**: settlers are rights-holders under UDHA and RA 10121 practice, not flood-control obstacles.

## YCLAP ethics rules

**Default rule: invited partnership only.** Informal settlement flood work is ethically high-risk (tenure insecurity, eviction politics, trauma, extractive research history). No cold outreach into settlements for photos, “needs assessments,” or prototype tests.

### Hard rules (non-negotiable)

1. **Invitation first** — Written or documented invitation from a legitimate community anchor: HOA / people’s organization, barangay (with community consent), established CSO/NGO partner, or LGU DRRMO/housing office that is **already** working *with* (not only *on*) residents. No “we found a community on Google Maps.”  
2. **No unsolicited entry** — Do not enter compounds, walk estero banks for content, or recruit respondents without prior organizer consent and a named host.  
3. **Do not produce eviction intelligence** — No household censuses, GPS rosters, “danger zone” name lists, or maps that identify remaining structures for clearance. Public secondary data only unless the community asks for *its own* planning tool under its control.  
4. **Consent is ongoing and informed** — Purpose, risks, who will see materials, and right to refuse/withdraw explained in plain language (and local language). Photos/voices of residents—especially children—require explicit consent; default to no identifiable imagery in pitches.  
5. **No savior narrative** — Residents are partners and knowledge-holders. Credit organizers. Avoid “we rescued / relocated / empowered them” claims.  
6. **Do not promise housing, land, or government action** you cannot deliver. Do not collect fees or “registration” money.  
7. **Minimize extractive research** — Prefer co-designed outputs that the partner keeps (playbook, IEC, drill kit). Share drafts before public pitch. No thesis-style extract-and-leave within the 5-week window without a clear community benefit.  
8. **Safety and do-no-harm** — Flood sites can be physically dangerous; follow host safety rules. Do not schedule activities that interfere with livelihood hours or disaster response. Stop if politics of demolition escalate.  
9. **Data protection** — Treat contact lists and stories as sensitive. No open publishing of personal data. University/ethics guidance applies when formal research methods are used.  
10. **Scope honesty** — Label student work as pilot / educational / support to existing CBDRRM—not as official DRRM, NHA housing, or climate-adaptation finance delivery.

### Preferred engagement path

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| 1 | Work from **public sources** and published cases until a partner invites you |
| 2 | Route through **existing** CSO, SK (if rooted), barangay BDRRMC, university extension, or LGU programme—not freestyle community hunting |
| 3 | Co-define a **tiny, useful** deliverable the partner names |
| 4 | Get **consent** for any field activity and for showcase use of stories/images |
| 5 | Return materials; debrief; no surprise media |

### Explicitly out of bounds for YCLAP student projects

- Demolition support, “voluntary” clearing campaigns, or content that shames riverside residents  
- Fake “People’s Plans” or forged consultation  
- Carbon-credit or offset schemes on informal settlement land  
- Payment of individuals for “poverty porn” interviews  
- Projects that require multi-year housing finance to “succeed”

### Ethical “green light” examples (still invitation-only)

- Co-refresh barangay flood early-action cards with BDRRMC  
- Support a partner CSO’s drill logistics or inclusive alert wording  
- Waste/estero clog pilot **requested** by HOA and aligned with SWM duties  
- Desk research + policy briefing that communities or allies can use (no field intrusion)
