**Agent:** YCLAP research (wave 3)  
**Scope:** Informal waste worker (IWW) numbers, income, gender, health, EPR / just-transition language, cooperatives; ILO / World Bank / local studies 2020–2026; Pasig / Quezon City / Marikina where available  
**Date:** 2026-08-07  
**Status:** Synthesis for YCLAP–Gargar justice framing

## Key findings

### 1. Scale and role

- **National headcount (order of magnitude):** Media, DENR-adjacent reporting, and sector briefs commonly cite **more than 100,000 informal waste workers** nationwide. This is a recurring estimate, not a PSA census line item; treat as a working figure, not a registry count.
- **What they do:** Itinerant waste buyers, street and dumpsite pickers/reclaimers, truck “jumpers” / *paleros*, unlicensed or semi-formal junkshop workers, and community MRF / zero-waste association members. They recover PET, HDPE, PVC, paper, cardboard, metals, and other high-value recyclables sold into junkshop → consolidator → recycler chains.
- **System dependence:** Only ~**39% of barangays** had materials recovery facilities in COA performance-audit framing (RA 9003 duty). Informal labor fills collection, sorting, and diversion gaps that formal LGU and private hauler systems leave open. Zero-Waste Month / circularity campaigns explicitly flag “integrating sustainability and circularity into the informal waste sector,” while workers still lack clear statutory labor protections under RA 9003 alone.
- **Metro Manila context:** NCR accounts for a large share of national solid waste (~22–27% of PH solid waste in EMB historical series for 2010–2020). Older literature placed large picker communities around Manila dumps (e.g. historical “~12,000 in Manila” dump-picker order-of-magnitude in Medina/WIEGO-type reviews). Contemporary Metro reality is multi-node: street/itinerant picking, junkshops, LGU/private MRFs, and community associations rather than a single Smokey Mountain–style dump economy.
- **E-waste note (Metro-heavy):** Recent circular-economy e-waste work describes Metro Manila as the national e-waste / informal recycling epicenter (~25% of national e-waste), with ~80% of discarded electronics handled informally—overlapping the same IWW / junkshop ecosystem.

### 2. Income ranges

Income is **highly variable** by city, materials mix, access to transport (*kolong-kolong*, tribike), buyer lock-in, and cooperative membership. Published 2020–2026 figures:

| Source / setting | Finding | Caveat |
|------------------|---------|--------|
| Context / Eco-Business (2025), national narrative | **>100k IWWs; some earn < US$1/day** | Journalistic synthesis; lower tail |
| Evergreen Labs (2024) | Many live **below poverty line**; earn **less than half daily minimum wage** | Advocacy brief, not city-stratified survey |
| Fair Circularity / ASKI living-income case (Talavera, Nueva Ecija, July 2024; n=40) | Full-time worker equivalent earnings reported as **Php 84.53/hour** average (coop **Php 88.55**; independent **Php 81.84**; range **Php 60.38–100.63**). Plastics ~**25%** of revenue. **Living income** for family of 4: **Php 23,864/month** (~US$411 at ₱58/$). Benchmarks (Php/FTWE/month): extreme poverty 2,034; WB poverty line 3,454; min wage 15,677; formal waste worker avg 14,820 | **Provincial sample, not Metro Manila**; hourly framing is study methodology (FTWE). Most pickers still **below living-income** once family needs, food insecurity, and instability are priced in |
| Same study — hardship | **90%** worried about food adequacy; **92.5%** cannot eat healthy/nutritious food; **45%** skipped meals; **95%** cannot survive 1 week–1 month without revenue | High precarity even where vehicles/prices known |
| Community fee model (Dumaguete women workers, Context 2025) | **Php 50/household/month** collection fee; workers also pay **Php 3/sack** to government when operating outside formal authorization | Illustrates thin margins + legal friction (RA 9003 unauthorized removal of recyclables) |

**Takeaway for Metro work:** Do not assume a single “daily rate.” Design around **price transparency at junkshop / consolidator**, **buyer lock-in**, and **cooperative vs independent** spreads. Living-income and formal-sector benchmarks (not only minimum wage) are the right justice yardsticks.

### 3. Gender

- **Women are central, often undervalued.** Sector narratives and gender analyses (Ocean Conservancy / GA Circular; Rappler/WUNRN 2022; Evergreen 2024) place **women (and children)** heavily in informal picking, sorting, and small junkshop work. Domestic-care loads constrain scale and hours; men more often take truck-based collection, heavier hauling, and higher-value materials.
- **World Bank *People and Plastics*** (synthesis on IWWs in plastic value chains, ~2023–24): among interviewed IWWs across focus countries, **~67% women overall**, but **lower in the Philippines (~46%)** and Indonesia (~34%)—i.e. PH informal waste work is **gender-mixed**, not female-only, with women concentrated at lower-pay sorting/picking nodes.
- **Occupational split (Ocean Conservancy gender study, PH among cases):** Women under-represented as formal recycling collectors (~8% F in one PH table) and truck/collection roles; better representation in street sweeping (formal) and **junkshop ownership/operation (~36% women)**; dump-site access historically brought women and children into mixed-waste recovery (e.g. Tondo).
- **2025 gender/socioeconomic analysis (Chan et al., PMC):** Female waste pickers from lower SES face **worse working environments, lower income, and higher vulnerability**—“twice as vulnerable / invisible” framing remains policy-relevant.
- **Organizing:** Women-led associations are visible models (e.g. Tagumpay 83Zero Waste, Manila Barangay 830; Dumaguete Women Waste Workers Association; PNWWA leadership).

### 4. Health and safety risks

Documented / repeatedly reported risks:

- Exposure to **mixed waste, pathogens, sharps, chemical residues, and toxic fumes** (including from open burning of plastics).
- **Respiratory illness** (including serious lung disease in some reported cases), skin infections, injuries, gastrointestinal disease; limited PPE (gloves, boots, masks) often self-financed.
- **No or weak health insurance / social protection** for most informal workers; DENR-EMB (2025) explicitly notes health risk from hazardous materials **and** exclusion from social welfare benefits.
- **Food insecurity and housing stress** compound occupational risk (Fair Circularity 2024 sample).
- **Child labor** remains a documented shadow risk where households depend on scrap income (Evergreen / Eco-Business child-scavenger reporting).
- DOLE **Institute for Labor Studies (ILS) 2023** just-transition study: across six pioneering zero-waste / transition sites, **decent-work deficits** on occupational safety and health, hazard pay, insurance, contractual precarity, and missing social dialogue—even where “green” jobs expand.

### 5. EPR and just-transition language (2020–2026)

| Instrument / actor | What it says / does for IWWs |
|--------------------|------------------------------|
| **RA 9003** (Ecological SWM Act) | Framework for segregation, MRFs, LGU plans; **no full labor code for informal sector**. Unauthorized removal of recyclables can criminalize informal recovery. |
| **National Framework Plan for the Informal Sector in Solid Waste Management** (NSWMC, ~2009; still referenced) | Long-standing national plan to integrate IWS into formal SWM; implementation uneven at LGU level. |
| **RA 11898 — EPR Act of 2022** | Obliged enterprises (assets > Php 1B) must recover plastic packaging (targets ramp **20% (2023) → 80% (2028)**). Law language encourages participation in **product waste recovery programs**; EcoWaste Coalition / PNWWA argue success requires IWW seats in PRO design, NSWMC-related spaces, and fair compensation—not only corporate recovery tallies. |
| **NSWMC Resolution No. 1655 (2024)** | Mandates LGUs to **integrate IWW support programs into 10-year SWM plans**—institutional recognition lever. |
| **DENR leadership language (2023–2025)** | “Do not leave waste workers behind”; integrate IWS into value chain; transform collection/sorting into **formal activities and enterprises supported under EPR**; reskill/upskill for circular economy / just transition. 2024: lectures for ~**200 IWWs** in NCR, Region III, Region IV-A on EPR/ESWM. 2025: grants for **weighing scales to 50 Quezon City junkshops**; training on org buildout, business planning, financial literacy; connect to obliged enterprises. |
| **Magna Carta for Waste Workers** | PNWWA (12 orgs, 1,000+ members, founded Feb 2024) drafted 10 demands (labor standards, hazard pay, health insurance, safe conditions, job security, just compensation, policy participation, right to organize, social protection, training). **SBN-2636** filed Apr 2024 (Legarda); **refiled 20th Congress Sep 2025**. DENR publicly welcomed formalization of rights/benefits. |
| **DOLE-ILS (2023)** | Explicit **ILO Just Transition Guidelines** frame: green/circular expansion without formalization, OSH, social protection, and social dialogue reproduces precarity. |
| **UN-Habitat / global just-transition literature** | *Leaving no one behind* (IWRS); WIEGO / GAIA: circularity without waste pickers is not just; cooperatives + EPR fee flows as inclusion tools. OECD-type risk: **poorly designed EPR can sideline informal workers** if PROs and large recyclers capture volume without fair offtake. |

**Just-transition test for any PH circular / EPR scheme:** recognition (IDs, plan inclusion) + **income floor / fair price** + PPE & health cover + **voice** (barangay ESWM committees → PRO → national) + cooperative bargaining power. Rhetoric is ahead of delivery; workers report **exclusion from EPR rulemaking** and opacity of **plastic-credit / recovery-market** valuations.

### 6. Cooperatives, associations, and organization

- **Cooperative premium:** Fair Circularity PH case—cooperative members earn slightly more than independents; 40% of sample were coop members vs 60% independent. Global evidence (WIEGO/Medina lineage; Systemiq living-income work) links organization to better prices, safety, and policy voice.
- **Philippine National Waste Workers Alliance (PNWWA), 2024:** National coalition; Magna Carta vehicle; GAIA Asia Pacific / Mother Earth Foundation / EcoWaste Coalition ecosystem.
- **Community zero-waste associations:** Tagumpay 83Zero Waste Association (Manila riverside barangay)—street sweepers, drivers, creek rangers, junkshop; UN-Habitat training/funding; women leadership; services ~5,700 residents + nearby villages/schools.
- **LGU / NGO models:** Mother Earth Foundation zero-waste barangay training (e.g. Dumaguete pathway); Plastic Bank collection-member model (cash + social benefits, blockchain tracking—private pathway into EPR-aligned recovery).
- **Policy push:** EPR advocacy repeatedly lists **help form cooperatives**, register workers, fair pricing, and direct partnerships with obliged enterprises.

### 7. Pasig / Quezon City / Marikina (local notes)

| LGU | Relevant signal (2020–2026 sources) |
|-----|-------------------------------------|
| **Quezon City** | Dense junkshop / recycling-chain node. World Bank SWM assessment interviews: junkshops accept PET/HDPE/PVC-type recyclables; **reject many SUPs** (straws, styrofoam, sachets)—income ceiling for pickers. **DENR-EMB 2025:** weighing-scale grants to **50 QC junkshops** + IWW training; EPR market-linkage intent. PNWWA Magna Carta launch framed from QC. Photojournalism of women sorting PET in QC junkshops widely used in 2024–25 coverage. |
| **Pasig** | Multiple MRFs (World Bank SWM: **~6** LGU-associated + history of **mechanized MRF** processing mixed waste under private contractors IPM then Eco-Waste; annual contract renewals noted as investment risk). Strong formal SWM apparatus coexists with informal recovery at Levels 1–4 (household → junkshop → consolidator → disposal-site picking). Relevant for **Gargar** as a high-visibility, multi-MRF city where price transparency and informal–formal handoff matter. |
| **Marikina** | Central MRF used for **recyclables recovery and composting** (World Bank SWM sector assessment). Smaller, more integrated municipal recovery footprint than Pasig’s multi-facility mix—useful contrast case for co-op / MRF partnership design. |
| **Metro Manila shared** | Diversion improved over 2010s with MRF growth (ADB SWM notes diversion rise e.g. toward ~42% from ~34% earlier baseline—method-dependent). Informal sector still critical for residual recyclable recovery junkshops refuse (low-value multi-layer, sachets). Food-market studies (CGIAR/related Metro work) flag potential triple-win if market admin coordinates with scavengers/junkshops on special waste. |

**Gap:** No high-quality **public, city-stratified IWW census** for Pasig / QC / Marikina 2020–2026 was found in open sources. City claims should use DENR registration drives, junkshop maps, and partner orgs (EcoWaste, MEF, PNWWA locals) rather than invent headcounts.

## YCLAP–Gargar implication (justice framing)

**Gargar is not only a scrap-to-value or diversion product; it is a climate-justice interface for informal waste labor.**

1. **Name the workforce.** Metro diversion and EPR recovery targets rest on IWWs who are still mostly **unregistered, underpaid, and unprotected**. Student projects that count kilograms without naming collectors reproduce extractive circularity.
2. **Price transparency is the justice feature.** Opaque junkshop and plastic-credit valuations are a stated worker grievance. Gargar should surface **fair, visible rates** and reduce information asymmetry between picker, junkshop, and obliged-enterprise offtake—not only maximize recovered kg for a brand.
3. **Prefer cooperative / association paths.** Where possible, design offtake, training, and PPE through **barangay associations, coops, or PNWWA-aligned groups** (Tagumpay-type models), not only individual spot-buying that locks pickers to one buyer (30% lock-in in ASKI sample).
4. **Gender-aware ops.** Women dominate sorting and small-scale recovery but earn less and carry care loads. Schedule, collection radius, material focus, and leadership seats must not default to male truck/hauler norms. Child labor is a red line—no “family scrap” romanticism.
5. **Health as non-optional.** Gloves, boots, masks, first-aid, and referral to PhilHealth / LGU clinic pathways belong in pilot design cost—not CSR afterthought. Align messaging with Magna Carta demands (hazard pay, insurance, OSH).
6. **EPR without capture.** Use RA 11898 and NSWMC Res. 1655 as **hooks to partner LGUs and obliged enterprises**, while insisting EPR money and recovery claims **include** informal recovery partners. Avoid pure plastic-credit / co-processing narratives that erase workers (see main deep-research brief).
7. **City fit.** **Pasig** (multi-MRF, contractorized formal system), **Quezon City** (junkshop density + DENR scale grants), and **Marikina** (central MRF + composting) are concrete pilots where student teams can map informal–formal handoffs without inventing national policy. Start with **one barangay association + one junkshop cluster + transparent rate card**.
8. **Metrics that match justice.** Track: kg diverted; **Php/kg to collector**; % of volume via coop/association; PPE coverage; women collectors paid; worker seat in any EPR/LGU conversation—not carbon credits as the product.

**One-line justice claim for Gargar:** *Climate mitigation (less landfill methane / less waterway basura) only counts as just transition if the people who already pick, sort, and sell get safer work, fairer prices, and a voice under EPR.*
